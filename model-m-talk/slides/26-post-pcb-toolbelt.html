測ってから直す Measure first. Form a hypothesis. Rework second.
Once the boards arrive, debugging becomes physical. Start with a multimeter: with power off, check continuity and look for shorts; with power on, measure every rail under the real load. A logic analyzer answers digital questions cheaply and quickly—did I²C acknowledge, did the multiplexer enable, did the UART transmit, did chip select move? An oscilloscope answers the questions a multimeter averages away: ripple, noise, rise time, unstable clocks, Hall sensor waveforms, and a supply rail collapsing during activity. The soldering iron closes the loop by letting you repair a joint, swap a component, cut a trace, or add a bodge wire. For firmware, expose the programming and debug header before fabrication. ST-Link is common for STM32, but the correct tool depends on the MCU and protocol; J-Link and CMSIS-DAP probes are common alternatives. The next upgrades are a current-limited bench supply, good flux, solder wick, fine tweezers, magnification, and hot air. The method is more important than the shopping list: observe, form one hypothesis, make one change, and measure again. Never use resistance or continuity mode on a powered board, and understand the grounding of your oscilloscope before attaching its probe.
