Adding a part is easiest when the footprint already exists. Tin only one pad, hold the component with tweezers, reflow that pad so the part settles flat, and then solder the remaining pads. Revisit the first joint with flux if needed. If no footprint exists, classify the electrical change before touching the board. A component across two nodes—such as a decoupling capacitor, pull-up resistor, or part of an RC filter—can often connect to exposed pads or vias. A component in series requires opening the old path first, confirming it is open, and bridging the gap through the new part. A more complex addition belongs on a tiny adapter or perfboard, not as a loose airborne sculpture. Insulate it from the PCB and anchor it mechanically. Before soldering, check value, tolerance, polarity, pin one, voltage rating, power dissipation, and the exact node name at every endpoint. A decoupling capacitor only works well when its loop is short; a high-speed clock or sensitive analog signal may not tolerate a long flying wire. Afterward, inspect, check continuity and adjacent shorts, and power from a current- limited supply. Finally, photograph the repair and annotate both schematic and layout. The bodge proves the electrical change; revision B makes it repeatable. Closing thought: ordering the PCB was not the end of design. It was when the board started giving us evidence.
